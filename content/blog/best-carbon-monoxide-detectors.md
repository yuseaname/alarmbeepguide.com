## What CO Detectors Must Do (The Standard, Briefly)

Every residential carbon monoxide alarm sold in the United States should be listed to **UL 2034**, the safety standard for single- and multiple-station CO alarms. "Listed" is not a marketing claim; it means an independent testing laboratory evaluated the unit against a published standard and the manufacturer is authorized to mark the product accordingly.

Here is what UL 2034 requires in plain terms, based on the verified standard:

- **No alarm below 30 ppm.** Residential CO alarms are designed *not* to alarm at concentrations under 30 parts per million. Low-level exposure is real, but the standard deliberately avoids nuisance alarms at background levels.
- **Alarm thresholds engage from roughly 51+ ppm, scaled by exposure time.** The higher the concentration, the faster the alarm must sound. At higher levels the alarm responds in minutes; at lower levels near the threshold it may take longer. This time-weighted response is the core of the standard.
- **End-of-life signaling.** Modern listed alarms must tell you when the unit has reached the end of its service life, typically 7–10 years from manufacture.

Why does "listed" matter? Because a detector that cannot prove UL 2034 listing may not meet the timing and threshold requirements that make the alarm useful. When you shop, look for the UL mark on the box or the back label. If it is not there, keep looking.

## The Four Decisions Before Any Brand

Before you compare brands, decide on four specifications. These matter more than the logo on the front.

### 1. Power Architecture

| Power type | Pros | Cons |
|---|---|---|
| **Battery-only** | Simple, no wiring, works anywhere | Batteries must be replaced; risk of dead-battery gaps |
| **Plug-in with battery backup** | Continuous power, backup during outages | Occupies an outlet; backup battery still needs replacement |
| **Hardwired with interconnect** | Whole-home alerting; one alarm triggers all | Requires wiring; professional install often needed |
| **Smart (Wi-Fi)** | Phone alerts, remote status | Depends on power and network; not required for safety |

Match the architecture to your home. Renters often prefer plug-in or battery units. Homeowners with existing hardwired smoke alarms may want a hardwired CO detector that interconnects. If you are unsure what you have, our [Hardwired Smoke Detector Beeping? 6 Fixes Beyond Battery](/blog/hardwired-smoke-detector-beeping-troubleshooting) guide explains common wiring setups.

### 2. Display or No Display

A digital PPM (parts per million) display shows the current CO reading and, on many models, a **peak-level memory** that records the highest reading since the last reset. This is genuinely useful for diagnosing chronic low-level sources — a furnace that leaks a little, a water heater that backdrafts occasionally. Without a display, you only know the alarm is silent or sounding; you cannot see a slow climb.

If you have ever wondered "is there a small leak I cannot smell?" a display with peak memory is the feature that answers it.

### 3. Combination Smoke + CO vs CO-Only

Combination units detect both smoke and carbon monoxide in one device. They reduce the number of units on your ceiling and simplify maintenance. The trade-off: when one sensor reaches end of life, the whole unit is replaced, and the smoke and CO sensors may have different lifespans. For a deeper look at how these units signal problems, see [Combination Smoke + CO Alarm Beeping Patterns](/blog/combo-smoke-co-alarm-beeping-patterns).

### 4. Voice vs Tone

Some units announce "Warning: Carbon Monoxide Detected" in a spoken voice before or alongside the tone. This matters in households with children, elderly residents, or anyone who might not immediately understand a beeping pattern. Voice alerts are a feature, not a requirement — but for some households they are the difference between confusion and action.

## Sensor Types, Honestly

Most residential CO alarms use an **electrochemical sensor**: a small cell that produces a current proportional to the CO concentration. These are accurate, stable, and the industry standard. Some older or less common units use **biomimetic** or **metal-oxide** sensors, but these are far less common in current retail models.

Here is the honest part: for most buyers, sensor type should *not* be the deciding factor. The four decisions above — power, display, combination, voice — have a much larger effect on whether the detector fits your home and gets maintained. A UL-listed electrochemical detector from a reputable brand is a sound baseline; the architecture and features are what separate good fits from bad ones.

- **Power:** AC plug-in with battery backup
- **Display:** None
- **Sensor:** Electrochemical
- **Lifespan:** ~7 years

**Best at:** A no-frills, reliable plug-in CO alarm for bedrooms, hallways, or apartments where you want continuous power without paying for a display you may not use.

**Best at:** Diagnosing. The digital display and peak memory let you see the highest CO level recorded, which is invaluable when you are trying to find an intermittent source.

## Installation Reminders That Make Any Pick Work

A good detector fails if it is placed badly. Follow these essentials:

- Place at least one CO alarm on each level of the home and near sleeping areas.
- Keep units out of dead-air spaces, directly above fuel-burning appliances, and away from garages where exhaust can cause nuisance alarms.
- Avoid extreme temperatures and high humidity; these shorten sensor life.
- Replace the unit at end of life, not when it finally fails. See [When to Replace a Carbon Monoxide Detector](/blog/when-to-replace-a-carbon-monoxide-detector) for the full timeline.

## FAQ

{{< faq question="How many carbon monoxide detectors do I need?" >}}
At minimum, one on each level of the home and one near every sleeping area. Larger homes and homes with fuel-burning appliances on multiple floors may need more.
{{< /faq >}}

{{< faq question="Should I buy a combination smoke + CO alarm or separate units?" >}}
Combination units save space and simplify maintenance, but when one sensor reaches end of life you replace the whole unit. Separate units let you replace only what fails. Choose based on your home's layout and your tolerance for maintenance.
{{< /faq >}}

{{< faq question="Do smart CO detectors alarm without Wi-Fi?" >}}
Yes. A UL 2034-listed smart detector must still sound its local alarm when CO is detected, independent of your network. Wi-Fi adds remote notifications but is not required for the alarm to work.
{{< /faq >}}

{{< faq question="When should I replace my carbon monoxide detector?" >}}
Replace it at the end of its stated service life, typically 7–10 years from the manufacture date printed on the label. Do not wait for it to fail. See [When to Replace a Carbon Monoxide Detector](/blog/when-to-replace-a-carbon-monoxide-detector) for details.
{{< /faq >}}
