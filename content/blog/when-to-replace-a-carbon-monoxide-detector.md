A carbon-monoxide alarm does not have one universal replacement age. The correct timing is stated by the manufacturer for the specific product, often on the label, in the manual, or in the app for a connected device. Check the make, model, installation or power-up date, and any replacement date printed on the unit.

Do not troubleshoot a loud repeating CO alarm as an age question. Get everyone outside to fresh air, call emergency services from outside, and stay out until responders say it is safe. The [CPSC](https://www.cpsc.gov/Safety-Education/Safety-Education-Centers/Carbon-Monoxide-Information-Center/Carbon-Monoxide-Questions-and-Answers) provides this guidance for a sounding CO alarm.

## Replace the unit when its documentation says to

Replace a CO alarm when any of these apply:

- Its label or manual says it has reached the replacement age.
- The manufacturer identifies its chirp, display, light, or message as an end-of-life signal.
- The product reports a non-serviceable sensor or fault.
- It has physical damage, water damage, or a recalled defect that requires replacement.
- You cannot verify its age or model and the manufacturer cannot support it.

A fresh battery cannot restore an expired CO sensor. A test button checks basic circuitry, not whether the sensor will detect CO accurately.

## Make a simple alarm record

Write down the room, manufacturer, model, installation date, stated replacement date, and battery type for each alarm. That record helps you replace compatible units at the right time and gives a landlord, electrician, or manufacturer useful information.

Combination smoke/CO alarms need particular attention because the CO component may determine the replacement timing for the whole unit. Follow the product documentation rather than assuming the smoke-alarm timeline applies. See [Combination Smoke + CO Alarm Beeping](/blog/combo-smoke-co-alarm-beeping-patterns/) for model-aware signal guidance.

## Choose and install a replacement safely

Use a CO alarm that meets current safety requirements and install it according to the manufacturer instructions and local code. The CPSC advises CO alarms on every level of the home and outside sleeping areas, with the exact placement guided by the product instructions. If you are replacing a hardwired or interconnected alarm, use compatible equipment and get a qualified electrician when wiring is involved.

## FAQ

### How many years does a CO detector last?

The answer depends on the model. Use the replacement date and documentation for your alarm rather than a generic range.

### Should I replace a CO detector if it chirps after a new battery?

First make sure the sound is not an active CO alarm. Then check the model manual. A continuing documented end-of-life or fault signal means the unit should be replaced; a battery change alone cannot fix an expired sensor.

### Can a landlord replace an older CO detector?

Requirements vary by location and lease. Report the model, date label, and signal to the property manager promptly, especially if the alarm has reached its stated replacement date.
