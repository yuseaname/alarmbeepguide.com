# CO Detector 4 Beeps: Alarm Pattern, Safety Steps, and Next Moves

Four beeps from a carbon monoxide (CO) detector is a specific alarm pattern that requires immediate attention. Unlike the single chirp of a low-battery warning, 4 beeps indicates that the CO detector has measured dangerous levels of carbon monoxide in your home. This guide explains exactly what 4 beeps means, the safety steps to take right now, and how to prevent future CO events.

## What 4 Beeps Means

Four beeps from a CO detector indicates that the detector has measured CO at dangerous levels. The pattern is typically:

- 4 loud beeps
- 4-5 second pause
- 4 loud beeps
- 4-5 second pause
- (Repeating until CO levels drop or the alarm is silenced)

This pattern is standardized across all UL 2034-certified CO detectors in the US. The 4-beep pattern is specifically for carbon monoxide — it is not used for low battery, end of life, or other trouble alerts.

## What to Do Right Now

If your CO detector is sounding 4 beeps, follow these steps immediately:

### Step 1: Get Out of the Building

Do not investigate the source of the CO. Carbon monoxide is odorless and colorless. You cannot detect it without a CO detector. Every second of exposure increases your CO blood level.

Get yourself, your family, and your pets out of the building immediately.

### Step 2: Call 9-1-1

Once you are outside, call 9-1-1. The fire department has CO detection equipment and can identify the source of the CO in your home.

Do not call from inside the building. Move to a safe distance first.

### Step 3: Do Not Re-enter Until Cleared

Wait for the fire department to arrive. They will:
- Measure CO levels in your home
- Identify the source of CO
- Determine when it's safe to re-enter

Do not re-enter your home until the fire department tells you it's safe.

### Step 4: Seek Medical Attention

If anyone in your home is experiencing symptoms of CO poisoning (headache, dizziness, nausea, confusion), seek immediate medical attention. CO poisoning can be fatal.

Symptoms include:
- Headache
- Dizziness
- Nausea
- Confusion
- Fatigue
- Shortness of breath
- Loss of consciousness

## Common Sources of Carbon Monoxide

Carbon monoxide is produced by the incomplete combustion of fossil fuels. Common sources in homes include:

### Heating Systems

- Furnaces (gas, oil, or propane)
- Boilers
- Water heaters (gas)
- Fireplaces (gas or wood)
- Wood stoves
- Space heaters (gas, propane, or kerosene)

### Cooking Appliances

- Gas stoves and ovens
- Gas cooktops
- Charcoal grills (indoor use)

### Vehicles and Equipment

- Cars running in attached garages
- Lawn mowers (in enclosed spaces)
- Generators (in enclosed spaces)
- Power tools (in enclosed spaces)

### Other Sources

- Blocked chimneys
- Cracked heat exchangers
- Backdrafting from appliances
- Poorly ventilated combustion appliances

## Why CO Detectors Use 4 Beeps

The 4-beep pattern is standardized by UL 2034, the standard for residential CO detectors. The standard requires:

- **4 beeps** for CO alarm (with a pause between cycles)
- **Different sound** for low battery (usually 1 chirp per minute)
- **Different sound** for end of life (usually 2-3 chirps per minute)

This standardization means that regardless of brand, a 4-beep CO detector signals the same thing: dangerous CO levels.

## CO Levels and When Alarms Trigger

UL 2034 specifies when CO detectors must alarm based on CO concentration:

| CO Level (ppm) | Alarm Time |
|----------------|------------|
| 30 ppm | No alarm (below threshold) |
| 70 ppm | Within 1-4 hours |
| 150 ppm | Within 10-50 minutes |
| 400 ppm | Within 4-15 minutes |

A 4-beep alarm can trigger at any of these levels. The faster the alarm, the higher the CO concentration.

## Common Mistakes During a CO Alarm

### Mistake 1: Investigating Before Leaving

Do not try to find the source of CO before leaving. The CO can overcome you within minutes. Get out first.

### Mistake 2: Opening Windows Before Calling 9-1-1

Opening windows can dilute the CO, but you should still call 9-1-1. The fire department needs to verify that CO levels are safe before you re-enter.

### Mistake 3: Silencing the Alarm

Do not silence the CO alarm by pressing the test button or removing the battery. The alarm is telling you that CO levels are dangerous. Address the issue, not the alarm.

### Mistake 4: Assuming It's a False Alarm

CO alarms rarely false-alarm. If your CO detector is sounding 4 beeps, treat it as a real emergency until the fire department verifies otherwise.

### Mistake 5: Returning Inside Too Soon

Wait for the fire department to clear your home before re-entering. They have equipment that can measure CO levels precisely.

## After the Emergency: Identifying the Source

Once the fire department clears your home, work with them or a qualified technician to identify the source of CO. Common findings:

### Furnace Issues

- Cracked heat exchanger
- Blocked flue
- Failed ignition
- Improper venting

### Water Heater Issues

- Failed venting
- Backdrafting
- Blocked flue

### Other Appliances

- Gas stove left on
- Car running in attached garage
- Blocked chimney
- Portable heater in enclosed space

## Repair vs. Replace Decisions

After a CO incident, you may need to repair or replace the appliance that caused it:

### When to Repair

- The appliance is relatively new and the issue is minor (e.g., blocked flue)
- The repair can be made by a qualified technician
- The appliance passes inspection after repair

### When to Replace

- The appliance is old (15+ years)
- The heat exchanger is cracked
- The repair cost is more than half the replacement cost
- The appliance has had multiple issues

Always have a qualified technician verify that the appliance is safe before continued use.

## CO Detector Placement After an Incident

If your CO detector triggered, your placement may be inadequate. Consider:

### Where Was the Detector?

If the detector was far from the source of CO, you may need additional detectors closer to potential sources.

### Where Are Potential CO Sources?

Identify all combustion appliances in your home and place detectors near them:
- Furnace
- Water heater
- Gas stove
- Fireplace
- Attached garage

### How Many Detectors Do You Need?

NFPA 720 recommends:
- One CO detector outside each sleeping area
- One CO detector on every level of the home
- One CO detector near any combustion appliance

Most US homes need 3-5 CO detectors to meet these recommendations.

For full placement guidance, see our [Carbon Monoxide Detector Placement Guide](/blog/carbon-monoxide-detector-placement-guide/).

## Why Your CO Detector May Have Triggered

Even with proper detector placement, CO detectors can trigger if:

### CO Levels Briefly Spiked

Some appliances produce small amounts of CO during startup or shutdown. If the detector is too close, it may trigger during these brief spikes.

To prevent this:
- Move the detector farther from the appliance
- Use a detector with adjustable sensitivity
- Ensure proper ventilation around the appliance

### Detector Malfunction

In rare cases, the detector itself may malfunction. If the fire department finds no CO in your home, the detector may need replacement.

### Expired Detector

CO detectors expire after 5-7 years. An expired detector may false-alarm or fail to detect real CO. Check the manufacture date and replace if expired.

## CO Detector vs. Combination Smoke/CO Detector

If you have a combination smoke/CO detector, the 4-beep pattern is specific to CO. The detector will use different patterns for different events:

- **3 loud beeps:** Smoke detection
- **4 loud beeps:** Carbon monoxide detection
- **1 chirp every 60 seconds:** Low battery
- **2-3 chirps per minute:** End of life

For combination detectors, ensure that you understand which pattern means what. The 4-beep CO pattern requires immediate action, while other patterns may indicate maintenance issues.

## Long-Term Prevention Strategies

### Annual Appliance Inspection

Have a qualified technician inspect your furnace, water heater, and other combustion appliances annually. They can identify issues before they cause CO leaks.

### CO Detector Replacement

Replace CO detectors every 5-7 years. The CO sensor has a shorter lifespan than smoke sensors.

For more details, see our [How Often to Replace CO Detectors](/blog/co-detector-5-beeps-end-of-life/) guide.

### Proper Ventilation

Ensure all combustion appliances are properly vented. Blocked vents are a common cause of CO buildup.

### Never Use Outdoor Equipment Indoors

Never use grills, generators, or other fuel-powered equipment inside your home or garage. Even with the garage door open, CO can build up to dangerous levels.

### Install CO Detectors Near Sleeping Areas

Since CO symptoms can mimic the flu, you may not realize you're being poisoned while sleeping. Detectors near bedrooms ensure you're alerted even if symptoms are mild.

## What If the CO Detector Goes Off but There's No CO?

If your CO detector alarms but the fire department finds no CO, the issue may be:

### Detector Malfunction

The detector has an internal fault. Replace it.

### Detector Near a Brief CO Source

If the detector is too close to an appliance that briefly produces CO (gas stove, fireplace), it may trigger during normal use. Move the detector farther away.

### Detector Is Expired

An expired CO detector may false-alarm. Check the manufacture date.

### Detector Is Contaminated

Some household chemicals can contaminate CO sensors. If you've recently used chemicals near the detector, the sensor may be affected. Replace the detector.

## CO Poisoning Symptoms to Watch For

If you suspect CO exposure, watch for these symptoms:

### Mild Exposure

- Headache
- Dizziness
- Nausea
- Fatigue

### Moderate Exposure

- Severe headache
- Confusion
- Vomiting
- Loss of coordination

### Severe Exposure

- Loss of consciousness
- Seizures
- Cardiac arrest
- Death

If anyone in your home has these symptoms, get fresh air immediately and call 9-1-1.

## FAQ

### What does 4 beeps on a CO detector mean?

Four beeps indicate dangerous CO levels. Evacuate immediately and call 9-1-1.

### Is 4 beeps a false alarm?

CO alarms rarely false-alarm. If your detector is sounding 4 beeps, treat it as a real emergency until the fire department verifies otherwise.

### Why does my CO detector beep 4 times and then stop?

This usually means CO levels have dropped below the alarm threshold, but it can also indicate a brief CO spike followed by ventilation. The fire department should still be called to verify safety.

### How long does a CO detector alarm last?

CO detectors continue to alarm as long as CO levels are above the threshold. Once CO drops below threshold, the alarm stops. This is why you should still call 9-1-1 even if the alarm stops.

### What if my CO detector triggered and there's no emergency?

The fire department should verify. If they find no CO, the detector may need replacement. Common causes are detector malfunction, expired sensor, or contamination.

### Can CO detectors go off from cooking?

Yes. Gas stoves can produce small amounts of CO during use. If your CO detector is too close to the stove, it may trigger. Move the detector farther away or ensure proper kitchen ventilation.

### How do I silence a CO detector after an alarm?

You should not silence a CO detector during an alarm. After the fire department clears your home and CO levels are safe, press and hold the test button to silence the alarm.

### What's the difference between CO detector beeps and chirps?

CO detector beeps (4 loud beeps) indicate dangerous CO levels. Chirps (single short beeps every 30-60 seconds) indicate low battery or end of life. Different patterns, different meanings.

### Where should I place CO detectors?

Outside each sleeping area, on every level of the home, and near combustion appliances. See our [Carbon Monoxide Detector Placement Guide](/blog/carbon-monoxide-detector-placement-guide/) for full details.

### How long do CO detectors last?

5-7 years for CO-only detectors. 10 years for combination smoke/CO detectors (though the CO sensor may expire sooner). Replace after the manufacturer-stated lifespan.
