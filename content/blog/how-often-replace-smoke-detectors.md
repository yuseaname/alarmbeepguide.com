Smoke detectors need replacement on a regular schedule, not just when they stop working. The sensors degrade over time, and even a detector that passes its self-test may not detect smoke reliably after a certain number of years. This guide covers the replacement timeline for every type of smoke detector and the warning signs that mean you should replace yours now.

## The 10-Year Rule (Most Detectors)

The US standard for smoke detector replacement is 10 years from the date of manufacture. This is set by the National Fire Protection Association (NFPA 72) and adopted by virtually all US jurisdictions.

### Why 10 Years?

The sensors inside smoke detectors degrade over time. Specifically:

- **Photoelectric sensors:** The light source (usually an LED) dims over time. After 10 years, the light output is too weak to reliably detect smoke particles.
- **Ionization sensors:** The radioactive source (americium-241) decays over time. After 10 years, the ionization level is too low to detect smoke particles reliably.
- **Carbon monoxide sensors:** The electrochemical cell that detects CO has a finite lifespan. Most CO sensors last 5-7 years, shorter than smoke sensors.

Even with regular battery replacement and cleaning, the sensors cannot be restored to factory-fresh condition. After 10 years, the detector must be replaced.

### How to Find the Manufacture Date

The manufacture date is printed on the back of every smoke detector. Look for a label that says "Manufactured:" or "MFG:" followed by a date.

Common date formats:
- "2014-06" (June 2014)
- "June 2014"
- "EXP: 06/2024" (expiration date — June 2024)
- "Replace by: 2024"

If you can't find a date, check the model number on the manufacturer's website. The model number often includes a year code.

### What 10 Years Looks Like in Practice

If your home was built in 2015 and has the original smoke detectors, those detectors expire in 2025. Many US homes have detectors that are already past their expiration date.

If you moved into an existing home and don't know when the detectors were installed, check the manufacture dates on the back of each unit. This gives you a clear timeline.

## Different Lifespans by Detector Type

Not all smoke detectors last exactly 10 years. The actual lifespan depends on the type and usage.

### Smoke-Only Detectors: 10 Years

Standard smoke detectors (whether ionization or photoelectric) last 10 years from manufacture. This is the most common type and the basis for the standard replacement rule.

### Combination Smoke/CO Detectors: 5-7 Years for CO Sensor

Combination smoke and carbon monoxide detectors have two sensors. The smoke sensor lasts 10 years, but the CO sensor usually expires after 5-7 years. The detector may chirp to indicate CO sensor expiration before the smoke sensor expires.

If your combination detector chirps to indicate CO sensor expiration, you have two options:
1. Replace the entire detector (most common)
2. Replace the CO sensor module (only some models allow this; check your manual)

### Smart Detectors: 7-10 Years

Smart detectors like the Nest Protect and First Alert SC5 have additional electronics (Wi-Fi radio, microcontroller, speaker) that can fail before the sensor itself. Most smart detectors are designed to last 7-10 years.

For Nest Protect specifically, see our [Nest Protect End-of-Life](/blog/nest-protect-end-of-life-expiration/) guide.

### Heat Detectors: 10 Years

Heat detectors trigger when the temperature rises above a threshold (usually 135°F or 194°F). The thermal sensor has a similar lifespan to smoke sensors. Replace every 10 years.

### Carbon Monoxide-Only Detectors: 5-7 Years

CO-only detectors (without smoke detection) typically last 5-7 years. The electrochemical CO sensor has a shorter lifespan than photoelectric smoke sensors. Replace CO-only detectors every 5-7 years.

## Signs That Mean Replace Now

Beyond the standard timeline, certain symptoms mean you should replace your detector immediately, regardless of age:

### The Detector Chirps Every 30 Seconds

This pattern usually indicates end of life or sensor fault. See our [Smoke Detector Beeping Every 30 Seconds](/blog/smoke-detector-beeping-every-30-seconds/) guide for details.

### The Detector Fails a Self-Test

Press and hold the test button. The detector should sound the alarm briefly. If it doesn't, the speaker or electronics have failed. Replace the detector.

### The Detector Has Visible Damage

Cracked case, water stains, scorch marks, or physical impact damage all mean the detector must be replaced. Even if the detector still works, the damage compromises its reliability.

### The Detector Has Been Recalled

Check for active recalls on your detector's brand and model. Kidde has had multiple recalls in recent years. See our [Kidde Smoke Detector Recall Guide](/blog/kidde-smoke-detector-recall-guide/) for current recall information.

### The Detector Has Frequent False Alarms

If your detector chirps or sounds the alarm without smoke present, the sensor may be degrading. Common causes:

- Dust contamination
- Steam exposure (in kitchens or bathrooms)
- Insect infestation
- Sensor aging

Try cleaning the detector. If false alarms continue, replace the detector.

### The Detector Is More Than 10 Years Old

This is the most obvious sign. If the manufacture date is more than 10 years ago, replace it regardless of whether it appears to work.

## How to Set Up a Replacement Schedule

Don't wait until a detector chirps at 2 AM to think about replacement. Set up a proactive schedule.

### Method 1: The Manufacture Date Method

For each detector in your home:

1. Find the manufacture date on the back
2. Calculate the expiration date (10 years from manufacture)
3. Add a reminder to your calendar 6 months before the expiration date
4. Plan the replacement (purchase new detector, schedule installation)

### Method 2: The Install Date Method

If you know when each detector was installed (rather than manufactured), use the install date plus 10 years. If the detector is more than 5 years old and you don't know the install date, use the manufacture date instead.

### Method 3: The Whole-Home Replacement Method

Replace all detectors in your home at once. This is the easiest method because:
- All detectors expire around the same time
- You only need to plan once
- You can standardize on one battery type and brand
- It's easier to remember

If your detectors were all installed at the same time (e.g., during home construction or a major renovation), whole-home replacement is the most efficient approach.

### Method 4: The Smart Detector Method

Smart detectors like the Nest Protect and First Alert SC5 track their own expiration date and notify you through the app. Set up the app notifications and let the detector tell you when it's time to replace.

## Replacement Planning: A Real-World Example

Imagine a home built in 2010 with the original smoke detectors installed at construction.

**2010:** Detectors installed (manufactured 2009-2010, so already 0-1 years old)
**2019:** Detectors are 9-10 years old. Time to replace.
**2029:** If not replaced in 2019, detectors are now 19-20 years old — well past expiration.

The cost of replacing 7 detectors in 2019 is approximately:
- $25 per detector x 7 = $175 for basic battery detectors
- $40 per detector x 7 = $280 for 10-year sealed lithium detectors
- $120 per detector x 7 = $840 for smart detectors

Spread over 10 years, the cost is $17-84 per year, or $1.40-7 per month. This is one of the most affordable home safety investments.

## What to Do With Old Detectors

Don't throw old detectors in the regular trash. They contain electronic components and (in ionization detectors) small amounts of radioactive material.

### Recycling Options

- **Home improvement stores:** Home Depot and Lowe's often have e-waste recycling programs that accept smoke detectors.
- **Manufacturer mail-back programs:** Some manufacturers (First Alert, Kidde) offer mail-back recycling for old detectors.
- **Local e-waste facilities:** Most cities have e-waste recycling facilities that accept smoke detectors.
- **Hazardous waste facilities:** Ionization detectors contain radioactive material and should be disposed of as hazardous waste.

### Don't Put Them in Regular Trash

In most US jurisdictions, it's illegal to dispose of smoke detectors in regular trash because of the radioactive material. Check your local regulations.

### Don't Reuse Old Detectors

Even if an old detector appears to work, don't move it to another location or give it to someone else. The sensor is degraded, and the detector may not provide reliable protection.

## Buying Replacement Detectors

When it's time to replace, consider these factors:

### Budget Options ($10-25)

Basic battery-only detectors. Acceptable for rental properties and budget installations. Use alkaline batteries (1-2 year lifespan) or upgrade to lithium (5-10 year lifespan).

### Mid-Range Options ($25-50)

Hardwired photoelectric detectors or 10-year sealed lithium detectors. Better reliability, less maintenance. Recommended for most homes.

### Premium Options ($80-150)

Smart detectors with Wi-Fi connectivity and app alerts. Best for tech-savvy users who want remote monitoring. Examples: First Alert SC5, X-Sense smart detectors.

## Special Considerations

Some situations require more frequent replacement:

### Homes with High Dust or Pet Hair

If you have pets that shed heavily, or if your home is in a dusty area (near construction, dirt roads, etc.), the sensor may degrade faster. Consider replacing every 7-8 years instead of 10.

### Homes with Frequent Cooking or Steam

Kitchens and bathrooms generate steam and cooking residue that can contaminate smoke sensors. If your smoke detector is near a kitchen or bathroom, expect to replace it more frequently.

### Homes with Smokers

Tobacco smoke residue can accumulate on smoke sensors. If anyone in your home smokes indoors, replace detectors more frequently.

### Homes with HVAC Issues

If your HVAC system has issues with dust, mold, or poor filtration, the air quality throughout the home may be poor, accelerating sensor degradation.

### Rental Properties

Landlords should replace detectors between tenants. Even if the detector appears to work, it may not be reliable for a new tenant. Most states require landlords to provide working detectors at the start of each tenancy.

## What If Your Detector Doesn't Have a Visible Date?

Some older detectors have faded or damaged labels. If you can't find the manufacture date:

1. Check the model number on the manufacturer's website
2. Look for a manufacture date inside the battery compartment (some detectors have it there)
3. If you can't find the date, replace the detector — it's likely old

If you don't know when the detector was installed, assume it's older than you think and replace it.

## The 10-Year Rule Is a Minimum, Not a Maximum

The 10-year rule is a minimum standard. Some detectors may last longer, but you should plan to replace at 10 years to ensure reliable protection. Waiting beyond 10 years increases the risk of detector failure.

### Why You Shouldn't Wait

The sensor degradation is gradual. A 12-year-old detector may pass its self-test but not detect smoke as reliably as a 5-year-old detector. By the time the detector starts chirping or failing tests, the sensor has already been degraded for years.

### The Cost-Benefit Argument

A new detector costs $25-150. The protection it provides over 10 years is approximately $2.50-15 per year. Even if your current detector might last a few more years, the cost-benefit favors replacement at the 10-year mark.

## FAQ

### How often should I replace smoke detector batteries?

For alkaline batteries, replace annually. For lithium batteries, replace every 5-10 years. For sealed 10-year lithium detectors, the entire detector is replaced when the battery dies.

### What if my smoke detector is more than 10 years old but works fine?

Replace it anyway. The sensor may be degraded even if the detector passes its self-test. After 10 years, the risk of failure is too high to leave in service.

### Can I check the sensor to see if it's still working?

There's no consumer-grade test for sensor accuracy. The self-test only verifies that the electronics work, not that the sensor is detecting smoke reliably. Trust the manufacture date and replace accordingly.

### Do smoke detectors have an expiration date printed on them?

Yes. Most detectors have a "Replace by" date or manufacture date printed on the back. The replace-by date is 10 years after the manufacture date.

### Should I replace all detectors at once or one at a time?

If they're all the same age, replace all at once. If they're different ages, replace the oldest first.

### Can a smoke detector last 15 years?

Technically, a detector may function for 15+ years, but the sensor is no longer reliable after 10 years. Replace at 10 years for safety.

### What is the cost of not replacing a smoke detector?

The detector may fail to detect a fire, leading to property damage, injury, or death. The cost of a new detector ($25-150) is trivial compared to the cost of a house fire.

### Are smoke detectors required by law?

Yes, in all 50 states. The specific requirements vary by state and jurisdiction, but the basic requirement (at least one detector per level) is universal.

### How do I know if my smoke detector is hardwired or battery-only?

Hardwired detectors are connected to your home's electrical system. They typically have a wiring harness at the mounting bracket. Battery-only detectors mount directly to the ceiling with no wiring.

### Can I replace a smoke detector myself?

Yes, for battery-only units and like-for-like hardwired replacements. For new wiring or major upgrades, hire an electrician.
