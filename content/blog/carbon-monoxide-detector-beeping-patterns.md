A CO alarm is not a device to decode from memory. Alarm sounds vary by brand and model, and a loud repeating signal can mean a potentially deadly CO hazard. **If the alarm is sounding loudly and repeatedly, or you are not certain that it is only a documented maintenance chirp, get everyone outside to fresh air and call 911, your fire department, or local emergency services from outside. Do not re-enter until responders say it is safe.** This is the [CPSC guidance for a sounding CO alarm](https://www.cpsc.gov/Safety-Education/Safety-Education-Centers/Carbon-Monoxide-Information-Center/Carbon-Monoxide-Questions-and-Answers).

Once immediate danger is ruled out, use the make and model on the back of the alarm to find its manufacturer manual. That is the reliable way to identify its exact chirp, light, display, and replacement signals.

## The safe way to sort out a sound

| What you notice | Safe first response |
|---|---|
| Loud, repeating alarm; four-beep cycles on many CO models | Treat it as a possible CO emergency: go outside, call emergency services, and stay out until cleared. |
| A short chirp or beep at a regular interval | Check the exact model manual. It can indicate low battery, end of life, malfunction, or an error code. Do not assume the cause from timing alone. |
| A display message, flashing light, or unfamiliar sound | Read the manual or contact the manufacturer with the model number. If there is any chance it is an alarm event, leave first and seek emergency guidance. |

For a focused explanation of a repeating four-beep pattern, see [CO Detector 4 Beeps: Treat a Repeating Alarm as an Emergency](/blog/carbon-monoxide-detector-4-beeps/).

## Why the manual matters

Kidde's current CO support material illustrates why generic pattern charts are risky. On its models, four short beeps followed by silence indicate a dangerous CO level; a chirp can instead indicate low battery, end of life, malfunction, or an error code. [Check Kidde's guidance](https://www.kidde.com/support/carbon-monoxide-alarms/why-is-my-carbon-monoxide-alarm-beeping-or-chirping) only if you have a Kidde product; otherwise, use your own manufacturer documentation.

Do not remove the battery or unplug an alarming unit to make it quiet. A test button checks circuitry, not the accuracy of the CO sensor. Follow product instructions for testing, cleaning, and replacement.

## After a possible CO event

Carbon monoxide is colorless and odorless. Headache, dizziness, weakness, nausea, shortness of breath, confusion, vomiting, and loss of consciousness can be signs of exposure. Tell emergency responders if anyone has symptoms. If a fuel-burning appliance may be involved, do not operate it again until qualified personnel have inspected it.

The CPSC recommends CO alarms on every level of the home and outside sleeping areas, installed according to manufacturer directions. Learn more about [where to place CO detectors](/blog/carbon-monoxide-detector-placement-guide/) and [when a CO detector should be replaced](/blog/when-to-replace-a-carbon-monoxide-detector/).

## When it is appropriate to troubleshoot

Troubleshoot only after you have confirmed, using the exact manual and the circumstances, that the unit is not issuing a CO alarm. A fresh correct battery, a securely closed battery compartment, a documented reset procedure, the manufacture/replacement date, and any error display are useful checks. If the unit is at its stated end of life or reports a fault, replace it or contact the manufacturer.

## FAQ

### Is a chirp the same as a CO alarm?

No. A short chirp can be a maintenance or fault signal, while a loud repeating alarm may signal CO. The exact meanings are model-specific, so check the manual. If there is doubt about an alarm event, leave and seek emergency guidance.

### Can I check for the CO source before leaving?

No. The CPSC says not to try to find the CO source when the alarm sounds. Move outside first and call emergency services.
