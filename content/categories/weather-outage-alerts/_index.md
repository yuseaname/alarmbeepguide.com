Weather and public-safety alerts are designed to get attention quickly. Read the message, note the issuing agency and area, and follow the action it tells you to take. Do not rely on a generic tone description to decide whether an alert is urgent.

## Two official alert systems to know

**NOAA Weather Radio All Hazards** broadcasts National Weather Service warnings, watches, forecasts, and other hazard information around the clock. It requires a compatible radio or scanner; the [National Weather Service](https://www.weather.gov/nwr/) lists the seven broadcast frequencies and coverage information.

**Wireless Emergency Alerts (WEAs)** are short messages from authorized alerting authorities delivered to compatible phones in a targeted area. The [FEMA WEA guide](https://www.fema.gov/emergency-managers/practitioners/integrated-public-alert-warning-system/public/wireless-emergency-alerts) explains that no app or subscription is required. The message can include the hazard, timing, recommended action, and issuing agency.

## Use the right setup guide

- [NOAA Weather Radio alert setup](/blog/noaa-weather-radio-alerts-setup-guide/) explains receiver setup and SAME location codes. Use current NWS or local emergency-management information to confirm your county code and transmitter.
- [Emergency alert sounds on your phone](/blog/emergency-alert-system-phone-sounds/) explains what a WEA is and where phone notification settings may affect optional alerts. The exact menu labels vary by phone and operating system.

## When an alert arrives

Follow the message first, then check trusted local authorities, the National Weather Service, or local news for updates. Keep a backup power source for radios and phones when severe weather is possible. If an alert repeats unexpectedly, review the device or carrier guidance after the immediate situation; do not disable alerts broadly just because one tone was inconvenient.
